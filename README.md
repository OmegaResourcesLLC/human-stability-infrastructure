# Human Stability Infrastructure

> Version 0.1.1
>
> An open, exploratory framework for examining how instability develops across connected parts of human life and how people and systems might respond earlier, more clearly, and more responsibly.

**In plain language:** this is a set of foundations, reasoning roles, safeguards, examples, and feedback tools for seeing how pressures such as housing, work, transportation, childcare, health, income, and access to services can interact, so people and systems can coordinate support earlier without reducing a person to a score.

My dad used to tell me, "You're only as smart as the tools you know how to use, and if the tool doesn't exist, build it." I've carried that with me for a long time.

This project is my attempt to build something I wish had existed during one of the hardest chapters of my life. Back then, I didn't need another person telling me what was wrong. I needed someone who could see how everything was connected, help me understand what was starting to fall apart, and help me find a way forward before every problem became a crisis.

That is what this work is trying to become.

It is still early, and I expect parts of it to change as people test it, challenge it, and show me where it does not work. That is the point of opening it up. If you work in this space, have lived experience, build systems, support families, study human services, or simply see something I missed, I want you involved.

## Why this repository exists

People rarely experience one problem at a time.

A housing issue can create a transportation problem. Transportation can affect work. Work can affect income. Income can affect childcare, food, communication, healthcare access, and the ability to meet ordinary obligations.

Systems can also look resource-rich on paper while still leaving people with very little they can actually use. A program may exist, but that does not mean it is available when someone needs it or usable in that person's real circumstances.

That is why this work separates **resource presence, resource availability, and resource usability**.

A service can technically exist and still be out of reach because of a waitlist, staffing shortage, transportation problem, eligibility rule, limited hours, funding restriction, or failed handoff.

None of that is a new discovery. This project is an attempt to make those connections easier to see and discuss before several manageable problems become one large crisis.

The question behind the repository is whether we can get better at seeing those relationships early enough to coordinate useful support without turning a human life into a score, prediction, label, or automated decision.

## Four ideas behind the framework

These terms are related, but they are not interchangeable and they are not stages a person moves through.

- **Human Standability** looks at whether a person can maintain day-to-day function while pressure is building across different parts of life.
- **Organic Stability Intelligence** widens the view to the systems, environments, organizations, and relationships around that person.
- **Stability Intelligence** asks how meaningful changes in stability can be understood without pretending one number can explain a human life.
- **Stability Infrastructure** looks at the connective structures that may help fragmented systems coordinate, preserve continuity, and make existing resources genuinely usable.

The fuller definitions are in the [foundations folder](foundations/README.md).

These ideas overlap with established work in case management, integrated care, recovery capital, social determinants of health, prevention science, trauma-informed practice, systems of care, reentry, housing stability, behavioral health, workforce development, and other fields. This version names those areas of overlap, but it does not yet provide a source-by-source crosswalk. That is future research work, not something this repository should pretend is already finished.

## What this repository is

This repository contains conceptual foundations, reasoning roles, safeguards, field-feedback templates, and examples that can be used to examine stability-related problems from several perspectives.

The role files can be used as structured prompts with AI systems or as review guides for human teams. They are not autonomous authorities, professional substitutes, or decision makers. Their purpose is to help people ask better questions, identify missing information, challenge assumptions, and examine how different pressures may interact.

**If you use these roles with an AI tool, do not paste identifying participant information, case-file details, medical identifiers, legal identifiers, or other information that could reasonably identify a person.** Use synthetic or properly de-identified information and follow the privacy rules that apply to your setting.

The framework does not require every organization or community to use the same services, terminology, workflow, or technology. Local context matters, and any implementation should remain open to correction.

## What this repository is not

Human Stability Infrastructure is not a validated assessment instrument, diagnostic system, predictive model, clinical protocol, legal standard, sentencing tool, eligibility mechanism, supervision score, surveillance platform, or automated decision system.

The frameworks documented here remain exploratory and require independent research, field testing, criticism, and revision.

CORA may eventually provide one operational environment for some of these ideas, but CORA is not the same thing as Human Stability Infrastructure and should not be treated as proof that the underlying concepts work.

## What gets measured?

At this stage, **there is no Human Stability score**.

The framework can examine observable conditions and changes, such as whether transportation is available, whether a service can actually be reached, whether a handoff failed, whether a waitlist changed, or whether the participant reports that a pressure is getting better or worse.

Those observations may be counted, tracked, or compared when that is useful and appropriate. They should not be collapsed into a hidden score that claims to measure the worth, risk, compliance, or future behavior of a person.

If future versions introduce any formal measures, they should be transparent about what is being measured, open to participant correction, specific to the purpose, and tested before anyone claims they are valid.

## Status

Version 0.1.1 documents the current conceptual structure and gives reviewers something concrete enough to challenge.

The repository currently includes:

- working definitions for Human Standability, Stability Intelligence, Organic Stability Intelligence, and Stability Infrastructure;
- six principles intended to keep implementation centered on people rather than systems;
- five portable reasoning roles that can be used by people or AI-assisted workflows, all governed by the same shared safeguard baseline;
- one shared safeguard baseline that applies across every role, template, example, and future tool;
- field-feedback templates designed to capture failures and practical lessons without collecting identifying participant information; and
- one fully synthetic example showing how the pieces can be used together.

Several important questions remain unanswered. The framework has not established validated measures, causal relationships, predictive accuracy, universal intervention sequences, or evidence that using these materials improves outcomes.

Those are research questions, not assumptions this repository is allowed to make.

## The question I most want reviewers to answer

**Where does this break in real implementation?**

I am interested in places where the framework becomes confusing, impractical, duplicative, unsafe, insensitive to local realities, or simply wrong.

A useful failure report is more valuable to this project than polite agreement.

## A note about lived experience

Lived experience helped generate many of the questions behind this work, but one person's experience is not a universal model.

The repository should grow through the experiences of people who have received services, provided services, built systems, conducted research, managed programs, challenged institutions, and watched well-intended approaches fail.

No contribution becomes correct merely because it came from lived experience, professional credentials, institutional authority, or this repository's author. Claims should survive scrutiny regardless of where they came from.
