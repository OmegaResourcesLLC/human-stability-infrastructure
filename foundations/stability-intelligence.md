# Stability Intelligence

Stability Intelligence is an exploratory research direction concerned with understanding meaningful changes in stability over time.

The word intelligence is used here in the sense of developing better understanding from observations, context, relationships, and change. It does not mean that a computer should decide whether a person is stable.

A useful stability picture may eventually involve several forms of information, including what the person reports, what practitioners observe, what conditions are changing, what supports are available, and where uncertainty remains.

## What measurement means here

This project does not currently define a single Stability Intelligence score or Human Stability score.

Some individual conditions can be measured or tracked when that information serves a clear purpose. Examples might include whether a service is available, how long a waitlist is, whether transportation can reach an appointment, whether a handoff was completed, or whether a participant reports that a specific pressure is improving or getting worse.

Those observations should remain tied to their meaning. They should not be combined into a hidden number that claims to measure a person's worth, compliance, dangerousness, likelihood of failure, or future behavior.

If future research introduces formal measures, each measure should answer four basic questions: what exactly is being measured, why is it being measured, what evidence supports the measure, and how can a participant or reviewer challenge or correct it?

Until those questions are answered and the measure is properly tested, it should be treated as exploratory.

The important problem is not collecting as much information as possible.

The important problem is understanding enough of the right information to improve coordination without creating unnecessary surveillance or false confidence.

Stability Intelligence should therefore remain human-reviewed, evidence-aware, transparent about uncertainty, and limited to the minimum information reasonably necessary for the purpose.

The research question remains open: what information genuinely helps people and systems recognize meaningful change, and what information merely creates the appearance of understanding?
